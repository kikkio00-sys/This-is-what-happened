# Archive Content Style Guide

## Authority and Purpose

This guide is the permanent editorial authority for every story, biography, caption, historical explanation, family memory, and evidence note in *This Is What Happened*.

The archive's three governing documents have distinct responsibilities:

- The **Project Constitution** governs decisions.
- The **Design Bible** governs visual design.
- The **Content Style Guide** governs the written voice of the archive.

When an editorial choice is unclear, use this guide to decide how the archive should speak. Apply it within the decision-making boundaries established by the Constitution and alongside, rather than in place of, the visual standards in the Design Bible.

## Core Philosophy

This archive exists to help families understand their own history. It should feel like a knowledgeable family member saying:

> Let me tell you what happened.

The archive is:

- welcoming;
- evidence-based;
- conversational;
- respectful; and
- honest.

The archive is not:

- a museum;
- an exhibit;
- genealogy software;
- academic writing; or
- marketing.

We preserve people rather than display subjects. We explain evidence rather than present data for its own sake. We write for relatives who may arrive with deep knowledge, no prior knowledge, or strong feelings about what they find.

## Narrative Voice

Every piece of writing should sound warm, conversational, respectful, calm, trustworthy, and grounded in evidence. Use plain English. Prefer familiar words, direct sentences, and a natural rhythm. Write as a careful person speaking to another person, not as a system producing a report.

The archive should never sound:

- sensational;
- sarcastic;
- promotional;
- mechanical;
- embellished; or
- overly academic, unless directly quoting a source.

Even when a story contains tragedy, conflict, or mystery, do not manufacture drama. Let the known facts carry their own weight. Never reduce a person to a hardship, achievement, relationship, or record.

## Kitchen Table Voice

The approved emotional model is a conversation at the kitchen table. Imagine someone opening a box of old family photographs, letters, recipes, newspaper clippings, military papers, certificates, keepsakes, and memories while telling the story of what happened.

The speaker knows the material but does not show off that knowledge. They pause when the evidence runs out, explain unfamiliar details, make room for other memories, and handle painful subjects with care. Visitors should feel welcomed into a family conversation—never into a lecture.

## Story Structure

A story may follow this flexible flow:

1. **Introduction**
2. **Their Story**
3. **Family**
4. **Historical Context**
5. **Places**
6. **Photographs**
7. **Records**
8. **Questions Remaining**
9. **Sources**

This is a storytelling aid, not a required template. Evidence—not templates—determines which sections appear. Omit a section when it adds nothing, combine sections when that reads more naturally, and change the order when the person's story calls for it. Never create filler merely to complete the list.

## Writing Rules

### Write naturally

- Use a person's name and pronouns instead of repeatedly writing “the subject.”
- Prefer active, direct sentences when the evidence supports them.
- Explain relationships in the way a family member would understand them.
- Define an unfamiliar historical term the first time it appears.
- Use short paragraphs and descriptive headings to help readers follow the story.
- Use quotations sparingly and preserve their meaning and context.
- Attribute claims close to the words they support.

### Prefer human wording over database terminology

| Prefer | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| “We know from the 1940 census that Rosa was living in Dayton.” | “The census record returns Rosa's residence as Dayton.” |
| “Thomas and Ada married in 1922.” | “A spouse event was recorded in 1922.” |
| “This photograph may have been taken outside the family home.” | “Image location metadata is unknown.” |
| “No record found so far names his parents.” | “Parental fields are null.” |
| “Mary was the oldest of four children.” | “Mary occupied sibling position 1 of 4.” |
| “The passenger list gives her age as 19.” | “The source entity contains an age value of 19.” |

Use dates, names, and places as parts of a life, not as a stream of fields. Avoid labels such as “data,” “entry,” “asset,” “record object,” “user,” or “metadata” when ordinary family language will do.

## Historical Context

Historical events should support the person's story. Context should answer:

> How did this affect this person's life?

Explain only what helps readers understand a choice, constraint, journey, occupation, relationship, loss, or opportunity in that life. Connect the broader event back to specific evidence whenever possible. If the connection is only plausible, label it as such.

Historical context should never become the main story. Do not interrupt a biography with a general history lesson, and do not imply that a person experienced an event in a particular way without evidence.

## Evidence and Uncertainty

Match the strength of the language to the strength of the evidence. Cite or identify the basis for consequential claims and distinguish what is documented from what is remembered or inferred.

### Documented facts

Use direct language and name the evidence when useful:

- “Their marriage certificate records that Lena and Samuel married on June 4, 1931.”
- “The 1950 census lists the family at 18 Oak Street.”

Do not call something certain merely because it appears in one record. Records can contain errors, and a source establishes only what it actually supports.

### Likely conclusions

Signal a reasoned inference and explain its basis:

- “The family likely moved to Ohio between 1935 and 1940, based on the residences reported in those census records.”
- “This may be the same James Carter named in the city directory, but the identification is not yet confirmed.”

Use “likely,” “probably,” “may,” or “suggests” precisely. Do not use cautious language to disguise an unsupported guess.

### Family memories

Attribute the memory to a person, family tradition, interview, or written account:

- “Her grandson David remembers that she always sang while making bread.”
- “Family tradition holds that the brothers left home together.”

Do not silently convert a memory into a documented fact.

### Conflicting evidence

State the disagreement plainly, without forcing a conclusion:

- “Her birth certificate gives 1908, while later census records give 1909. No source found so far resolves the difference.”

When one account appears stronger, explain why rather than simply declaring a winner.

### Incomplete evidence

Name both what is known and what remains open:

- “We know that Arthur was in Chicago by 1926, but we have not yet found when or why he moved there.”
- “No photograph of Mae has been identified so far.”

“We do not know” and “not yet found” are responsible editorial conclusions. Never fill a gap with false certainty.

## Family Memories

Memories and documentary evidence can illuminate different parts of a life. Neither automatically overrides the other. A record may establish a date while a memory preserves how an event felt; a memory may identify a person whom a photograph does not name; either may also be mistaken or incomplete.

Present both respectfully and identify each for what it is. If they conflict, describe the difference without belittling the person who remembered it or dismissing the record without cause. Preserve the memory's meaning even when a detail cannot be verified. Invite further evidence when it may help resolve the question.

## Biography Openings

Begin by helping readers meet the person and understand why their story matters. A birth date may belong in the opening, but it should not be the opening's only purpose.

Effective openings include:

- “For more than forty years, Clara Webb was the person relatives called when they needed a place to stay. Her story connects the family's move north with the home they built in Detroit.”
- “Elias Morgan left few records behind, but the letters he sent during the First World War reveal a young father determined to return to his family.”
- “The recipe card says only ‘Aunt June's cake.’ Behind that familiar name was June Harris, a teacher, sister, and keeper of the family's holiday traditions.”

Avoid an opening that reads only, “John Smith was born on March 2, 1910, in Albany, New York.” Follow chronology when it serves the story, not by default.

## Photograph Captions

A caption should help a reader understand the photograph without overstating what is known.

- **Names:** Identify people from left to right when possible. Use full names on first mention and make clear when an identification is uncertain.
- **Dates:** Give a known date. Otherwise use honest ranges or terms such as “about 1945,” “probably late 1940s,” or “date unknown.” Do not infer a precise date from appearance alone.
- **Places:** Name the location and its relationship to the image when known. Write “possibly outside the Oak Street home” when uncertain.
- **Contributors:** Credit the person or collection that shared the original, using the contributor's preferred wording and subject to privacy wishes.
- **Uncertainty:** State “unidentified,” “believed to be,” or “possibly” in the caption itself; do not hide uncertainty in a separate note.
- **Restoration:** Disclose meaningful alterations. For example: “Digitally restored to reduce fading and repair a tear; no people or objects were added.” Routine resizing or file-format changes do not need a disclosure.

Example: “From left: Ruth Carter, an unidentified child, and possibly Ruth's sister Helen, outside the Carter home in Toledo, about 1948. Shared by Daniel Carter. Digitally restored to reduce fading.”

Do not describe assumptions as visual facts, and do not use captions merely as filenames or inventory labels.

## Document Introductions

Introduce a historical record by explaining what it contributes to the story. Use ordinary words before specialized terms, and connect the document to a person, moment, or question.

Prefer: “This passenger list places Sofia and her two children aboard the *Carpathia* in May 1912. It is the earliest record found so far of their journey to New York.”

Avoid: “Artifact 003 is a primary-source manifest accessioned in the migration collection.”

Mention legibility, missing pages, transcription choices, or disputed readings when they affect interpretation. A document is evidence in a story, not a technical archive object displayed for its own sake.

## Inviting Contributions

Invite relatives and visitors to strengthen the archive with memories, photographs, corrections, and documents. Requests should be specific, gracious, and free of pressure.

Approved forms include:

- “Do you remember this day differently? We would be grateful to hear your memory.”
- “If you can identify anyone in this photograph, please share their name and how you know.”
- “Have you found a record that corrects this date? Please send the record or tell us where it can be found.”
- “If your family has a related letter or document, you are welcome to tell us about it.”

Never promise or imply immediate publication. Explain that contributions may be reviewed, verified, edited for clarity, discussed with the contributor, or withheld for privacy. Giving something to the archive does not by itself establish permission to publish it. Credit contributors according to their wishes and record any restrictions they provide.

## Living People

Protect living people more carefully than historical completeness requires. Publish only information that is appropriate, necessary to the story, and shared with suitable permission. Do not publish private contact information, precise home addresses, sensitive identifiers, private correspondence, medical information, financial details, or information that could create a safety risk.

Treat difficult family events, disputed relationships, adoption, parentage, health, sexuality, and other sensitive matters with particular care. Consent to contribute an item is not automatically consent to disclose every fact it contains. When the benefit of publishing does not clearly outweigh the intrusion, omit the detail, generalize it, restrict access, or wait. Honor reasonable correction and removal requests and follow the Constitution's governance process when interests conflict.

Children cannot provide meaningful consent on the same terms as adults. Use only the minimum identifying information necessary and obtain permission from an appropriate parent or guardian.

## Empty and Emerging Stories

A page with little information should still feel welcoming, intentional, and honest. Say what is known, explain why the person or item belongs in the archive, name what has not yet been found, and offer a respectful path for contribution.

For example:

> We know Anna Lewis was part of the Lewis family in Richmond, but no record found so far confirms her birth date or identifies her parents. Her story is here because she appears in the family photograph below. If you recognize her name or have another photograph, we would be grateful to hear from you.

Do not pad a short page with generic history, repeat the same facts, or speculate to make the story seem complete. An honest beginning is better than an invented ending.

## Editorial Checklist

Before publishing or substantially revising content, ask:

- [ ] Does this sound conversational?
- [ ] Would a grandparent understand this without specialized knowledge?
- [ ] Is the language warm, calm, and respectful?
- [ ] Does the opening explain why this person's story matters?
- [ ] Is each factual claim supported at the level the wording suggests?
- [ ] Are memories, inferences, and documented facts clearly distinguished?
- [ ] Is uncertainty explained honestly?
- [ ] Are conflicting and incomplete evidence presented fairly?
- [ ] Does historical context support the family story rather than replace it?
- [ ] Are photographs and records introduced as meaningful parts of the story?
- [ ] Are names, dates, places, contributors, alterations, and uncertainties stated clearly in captions?
- [ ] Is private or sensitive information about living people necessary and appropriately permitted?
- [ ] Would the family feel respected, including family members who remember events differently?
- [ ] Does the page avoid database, museum, academic, and promotional language?
- [ ] Does this invite future discoveries without promising publication?
- [ ] Have sources, quotations, credits, and contribution restrictions been recorded accurately?

## Final Principle

The archive exists to preserve real family stories with honesty, warmth, evidence, and respect.
