# Archive Information Architecture

## Purpose

This document is the practical site blueprint for *This Is What Happened*. It defines:

- what pages exist;
- how visitors move through the archive;
- how content types relate;
- what information belongs on each page; and
- how the structure can grow as material is added without redesigning the whole site.

It makes the decisions needed to begin visual studies. It does **not** approve final visual design, wireframes, routing, or implementation; perceptible choices still require the Visual Approval Gate.

## Visitor Priorities

The structure should help a visitor, in this order, to:

1. Recognize a family name.
2. Enter that family's story.
3. Find a person.
4. Understand how people are related.
5. Read a human story rather than a list of facts.
6. View photographs and records in context.
7. Understand what is documented, remembered, likely, or uncertain.
8. Move naturally to related people, places, stories, and sources.
9. Contribute additional information safely.

Every route must pass the **Grandparent Test**: a relative arriving without coaching can understand the page, read it comfortably, move forward and back, and recover from a mistake. It must also pass the **Kitchen Table Test**: navigation should feel like a knowledgeable relative guiding a conversation—using familiar names and relationships, revealing context when it helps, and never sounding like a database, museum label, or software tool.

## Primary Navigation

Keep the always-visible primary navigation to **Home, Families, People, Stories, Places,** and **Search**. These match the most common ways visitors will enter and browse the archive. Put **About** and **Contribute** in a clearly labeled secondary area in the header menu and footer; contextual contribution invitations may also appear on relevant pages. Home may be represented by the archive name on desktop, but the opened menu should label it explicitly.

On desktop, show the primary destinations in a consistent header, with About and Contribute available without dominating the family material. On mobile, show the archive name and one labeled **Menu** control. The menu opens as a simple vertical list containing all eight destinations; Search is a labeled item, not an unexplained icon. Keep the current page evident and preserve ordinary browser Back behavior.

## Homepage

The homepage welcomes visitors, explains in a few plain sentences that this is a living family archive, and makes the family map the primary invitation. The map itself is directly selectable; do not add a redundant **Open the map** button when selecting the map already performs that action. Beside or immediately after it, provide an equally understandable text list for visitors who prefer not to use the visual map.

Introduce the four lines with equal visual weight and similarly brief, evidence-led copy:

- **Zahner:** an invitation into the Zahner family's people, homes, work, movements, photographs, and memories.
- **Downing:** an invitation into Downing households and communities and, where supported, their factory work, farming, and other labor.
- **Blackwell:** an invitation into the Blackwell family's people, places, work, photographs, and shared stories.
- **Barrett:** an invitation into the Barrett family's people, places, work, photographs, and shared stories.

Final introductions should use known specifics, not generic filler. Downing must not be defined by court or prison history; difficult records may appear only in proportion and context within individual lives.

Below the main invitation, surface only a small, edited group of meaningful stories or photographs with human descriptions. Avoid statistics, collection counts as a feature, activity panels, software language, and large equal-weight grids. A visitor should see one obvious beginning and a few calm next choices, not a dashboard.

## Family Landing Page

Each family page is an edited welcome to a line, not a surname search result. It should answer who the family is, where and when its known story takes place, and what a visitor can explore next. Include, when evidence supports them:

1. Family name and a short human introduction.
2. Principal places and broad time span, with honest uncertainty.
3. A simple explanation of important branches and how they connect.
4. A small selection of key people, chosen as useful starting points rather than rankings.
5. Featured stories.
6. Meaningful places.
7. Selected photographs and records with context.
8. Clear paths to more people, stories, places, and the family map.

Lead with narrative and recognition, not alphabetical tables, identifiers, totals, or dense pedigree charts.

## Person Page

A person page should leave the visitor feeling they have met someone, not studied a subject. Use this standard order as a flexible editorial guide:

1. A plain return path, the person's name, and lifespan or an honest qualifier such as “about 1880–after 1940.”
2. Immediate relationships in words: parents, spouses or partners, children, and siblings when supported and appropriate to publish.
3. A short human introduction explaining why this life matters.
4. The life story, organized into readable chapters rather than database fields.
5. A timeline only when it genuinely clarifies sequence.
6. Places connected to the life and why each mattered.
7. Photographs with captions.
8. Records and evidence connected to nearby claims.
9. Historical context only when it helps explain this person's choices or circumstances.
10. Questions, uncertainty, and conflicting evidence stated plainly.
11. Related people and stories.
12. Human-readable sources.

Omit or combine sections when evidence is sparse. Never add filler to complete the template. Privacy and dignity govern information about living people and sensitive relationships.

## Story Page

A story is a narrative page about one person, several relatives, a household, migration, workplace, military experience, local event, family tradition, mystery, or documented turning point. Its title and opening should tell visitors why the story matters; the body should distinguish records, memories, inference, and open questions without breaking the conversational flow.

Show the families, people, and places involved near the opening or close to their first appearance. Place photographs and documents beside the part of the story they illuminate. End with sources and named onward paths to every central person, family, and place, plus a small number of genuinely related stories.

## Place Page

A place page answers **why this place mattered to the family**. Begin with that connection, the historical and current place name where useful, and the relevant period. Depending on available evidence, include family connections, period maps, homes, farms, churches, workplaces, cemeteries, schools, and concise community history. Link to related people and stories throughout or in a closing group.

Community history belongs only when it explains a family's experience there. Do not turn the page into a generic local-history article or imply a connection that evidence does not support. Sensitive present-day locations should be generalized when privacy or safety requires it.

## Photographs and Documents

Use a focused detail view with its own readable URL for significant photographs and documents. It may appear as an accessible overlay on larger screens only if the same information, keyboard operation, focus management, direct linking, browser Back behavior, and mobile experience are preserved. Otherwise open the dedicated view. The full original object—or an uncropped view that links clearly to it—must remain available without interrupting the story.

Every view should provide, as applicable:

- a human title or description;
- people shown, including unidentified or uncertain identifications;
- known or approximate date;
- place and its relevance;
- contributor, custodian, or source;
- uncertainty stated beside the affected information;
- disclosure of restoration, colorization, reconstruction, or other meaningful alteration;
- an accessible transcription for documents when feasible; and
- related people, stories, families, and places.

Preserve aspect ratios and evidence-bearing edges. Never present an altered image as the original or expose raw filenames and internal identifiers as visitor labels.

## Family Map

The family map is the archive's central exploration tool and the homepage's primary invitation. Visitors select the map itself to enter a family or person; a separate action button is unnecessary when it duplicates that behavior. It must:

- distinguish Zahner, Downing, Blackwell, and Barrett clearly and evenly;
- explain relationships and important branches in familiar family language;
- reveal details gradually rather than showing an exhaustive chart at once;
- lead directly to family and person pages;
- use visible controls and never depend on hover, drag, zoom, or color alone;
- provide a complete, nearby list alternative; and
- remain understandable and usable on a phone.

The map is an invitation and orientation aid, not a dense genealogy editor. Its final form, labeling, interaction, mobile treatment, and accessible visual equivalent still require Design Authority approval through the **Visual Approval Gate**.

## Relationships Between Content

Use this concise, implementation-neutral model:

- A **Family** contains branches and connects to many people, stories, places, photographs, and documents.
- A **Person** belongs to one or more family lines and may relate to many people, stories, places, photographs, documents, and sources.
- A **Story** may involve one or more families, people, places, photographs, and documents.
- A **Place** may connect to many families, people, stories, photographs, and documents for specific reasons and periods.
- A **Photograph** may show or connect to people, places, families, and stories.
- A **Document** may concern people, places, families, and stories and provide evidence for particular claims.
- A **Source** identifies where evidence or a memory came from and may support one or more claims; one claim may also rely on several sources.

Relationships may be documented, remembered, likely, disputed, or unknown. That status belongs with the relevant statement, not hidden in internal data.

## Search and Browsing

Search accepts ordinary words and checks names, families, places, approximate dates, story subjects, and photograph or document descriptions. Results group or label content in human terms—**People, Stories, Places, Photographs,** and **Documents**—and show enough context to distinguish similar names. Family pages, indexes, the map, and cross-links remain full browsing alternatives; search is never the only route.

Offer only useful, optional filters: content type, family, place, and broad date range. Display active filters plainly with an obvious way to clear them. Do not require advanced query syntax, exact spelling, or specialist genealogical terms.

A no-results page should say that nothing matched, preserve the query, suggest checking spelling or using fewer words, offer links to the four family pages and People index, and invite a contribution when the visitor may know something the archive does not. It must not imply that a person or record does not exist merely because it has not been added.

## Cross-Linking Rules

- Person pages link to supported parents, spouses or partners, children, siblings, family lines, and related stories and places.
- Story pages link to all central people, families, and places, plus photographs, documents, and sources used.
- Place pages link to the families, people, and stories connected to that place and explain each connection.
- Photograph pages link to identified or possibly identified people and to the stories, places, and families that give the image context.
- Document pages link to the people, stories, places, and claims the record helps support.
- Sources are linked or cited beside supported claims and described fully in a source area; access restrictions or unavailable originals are explained.
- Family pages link back to the family map and onward to curated people, stories, places, and records.

Every detail page ends with a named return path and at least one relevant next destination when one exists. When none exists, offer its family page or a contribution invitation rather than an unexplained dead end. Do not manufacture weak “related” links merely to fill space.

## Breadcrumbs and Orientation

Every page shows a clear title, its content type in ordinary language when needed, and a visible return path. Use short, human breadcrumbs such as **Home / Downing family / William Alfred Downing**, not labels such as “Person record” or internal collection codes. A person connected equally to several lines should not be forced into a false single-family hierarchy; use **Home / People / [Name]** and show all family connections nearby.

Family context should be named in the introduction or relationship area and remain recognizable in related links. Long pages may include a simple **On this page** list. Return links name their destination—**Back to the Downing family**—and related groups explain what exists next.

## Mobile Navigation

Mobile is the complete archive experience:

- no hover-only interactions or hidden gesture requirements;
- touch targets at least 44 by 44 pixels, with comfortable spacing;
- short, plain labels and one simple vertical menu;
- a readable family-list alternative beside the map experience;
- no horizontal scrolling for primary content, relationships, tables, timelines, or evidence;
- relationships restated in words and stacked in a meaningful reading order;
- visible gallery and map controls that do not rely on swiping;
- comfortable type, contrast, focus states, and keyboard access; and
- headings, return paths, and current family context retained on small screens.

Dense branches should collapse into progressive, selectable groups or the text list rather than shrink into illegibility.

## Empty and Incomplete Pages

Sparse pages remain intentional: say what is known, why the person or item belongs, and what remains open. Apply these patterns:

- **No photograph:** use a dignified written opening; do not use a generic silhouette that could be mistaken for the person.
- **One surviving record:** show it in context, explain exactly what it supports, and do not stretch it into a full biography.
- **Incomplete dates:** use “about,” a range, “before/after,” or “date not yet known.”
- **Uncertain relationships:** name the proposed relationship and why it is uncertain; do not draw it as settled.
- **Conflicting evidence:** present the accounts and sources fairly, explaining why one may be stronger without silently erasing the other.
- **Unfinished research:** label questions as open and distinguish “not yet found” from “did not happen.”

Never invent material, generic history, or precision to make a page appear complete. Where useful, close with a specific, gracious invitation such as asking for an identification, memory, correction, or record, with a reminder that submissions are reviewed.

## Contribution Journey

No account is required for the initial contribution path.

1. A visitor selects **Contribute** globally or a specific invitation on a person, story, photograph, or document.
2. The page explains what can be shared: names, corrections, memories, photographs, documents, and identifications. A contextual link carries the relevant page title when possible.
3. The visitor provides a description, how they know it, optional files, preferred credit, contact details for follow-up, and any privacy or publication restrictions. Collect only what is necessary.
4. Before sending, plain language explains that submission is not publication permission, sensitive details should be avoided, and every contribution is reviewed for evidence, rights, dignity, and privacy.
5. A confirmation says the archive received the material, sets no promise of publication, and explains that a steward may ask questions.
6. A steward reviews, verifies where possible, discusses edits or permissions when needed, and only then publishes, restricts, or declines the material.

Provide a clear correction/removal contact path. Do not expose submissions publicly, enable public commenting, or build a complicated account workflow at this stage.

## URL Guidance

Prefer stable, lowercase, readable words:

```text
/
/families/
/families/zahner/
/families/downing/
/families/blackwell/
/families/barrett/
/people/
/people/william-alfred-downing/
/stories/
/stories/a-descriptive-story-title/
/places/
/places/dayton-ohio/
/photographs/descriptive-photograph-name/
/documents/descriptive-document-name/
/search/
/about/
/contribute/
```

Use disambiguating words when names repeat and preserve old links with redirects if titles change. Avoid visitor-facing numeric record IDs, dates masquerading as hierarchy, and internal collection codes. These are content and readability recommendations, not final technical routing requirements.

## Initial Release Scope

The smallest complete first release includes:

- a welcoming homepage;
- the directly selectable family map, or an approved accessible visual equivalent, plus its family list;
- balanced landing pages for Zahner, Downing, Blackwell, and Barrett;
- one strong featured person page: William Alfred Downing;
- one or more complete example stories;
- the essential photographs and records needed by those pages, with detail views and sources;
- About and Contribute pages;
- basic primary/mobile navigation, breadcrumbs, cross-links, and a simple People browsing route; and
- search if a useful basic search can be delivered without delaying the narrative foundation; otherwise a clearly browseable People route serves the first release and Search follows promptly.

The release does not need every known ancestor, place, photograph, or record. It should feel complete because every published route is understandable, sourced, connected, mobile-ready, and free of placeholder promises, while the repeatable page types allow incremental growth.

## Deferred Features

These should not block the initial release:

- visitor accounts or profiles;
- advanced search syntax and multi-facet filtering;
- automated or public family-tree editing;
- complex family-map animation, pan/zoom, or speculative relationship rendering;
- public commenting or discussion threads;
- bulk visitor uploads;
- sophisticated administrative dashboards;
- personalized recommendations, notifications, or saved lists; and
- exhaustive digitization or publication of every known record.

Defer any feature that makes software more visible without making the family story easier to understand.
